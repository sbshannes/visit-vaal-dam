The flying-boat story

Flying boats on the Highveld

Once upon a time you could fly from Southampton to the Vaal Dam, step off a flying boat at Deneysville, and be in Johannesburg by evening.

Picture it: a big, silver, four-engined flying boat drops out of the Highveld sky, skims low over the brown grassland and touches down on the Vaal Dam in a long plume of spray. It taxis in towards the shore, and passengers step ashore at Deneysville, about 1,500 metres above sea level and hundreds of kilometres from the nearest ocean.

For a short, glamorous chapter of South African aviation history, this really happened.

The Empire boats

In the 1930s Britain’s Imperial Airways ordered a fleet of large four-engined Short “C-class” or Empire flying boats to carry mail and passengers along the routes of the Empire. In June 1937 the Empire boat Canopus flew the first through service from Southampton to South Africa. That pre-war route came down the east coast of Africa and ended at Durban, where the boats landed in the bay.

Some local accounts say flying-boat mail flights reached the Vaal Dam as early as 1938. We have not been able to confirm a regular pre-war service to the dam, so we tell that part of the story as local memory rather than fact.

The war years

During the Second World War, Imperial Airways’ successor, the British Overseas Airways Corporation (BOAC), kept flying-boat services going across Africa and the Indian Ocean. Official RAF photographs from the early 1940s show Empire boats such as Cathay taking off from the Vaal Dam, which BOAC then described as its South African marine terminus and staging post. Training facilities used by flying-boat crews during the war were later enlarged for the peacetime service.

The Springbok service

The golden age came after the war. On 4 May 1948 BOAC launched a passenger and mail service from Southampton to the Vaal Dam using the new Short Solent flying boats, a service promoted as part of BOAC’s “Springbok” route to South Africa. The first flight was flown by the Solent Severn.

There was no night flying, so the journey took about four and a half days, following the Nile and the great lakes of East Africa with stops at Augusta in Sicily, Cairo, Luxor, Khartoum, Port Bell on Lake Victoria and, most famously, Victoria Falls, where the boats landed on the Zambezi above the falls. The final leg brought them to the Vaal Dam, about 110 kilometres (68 miles) from Johannesburg.

At Deneysville there was a flying-boat base with a terminal building and support facilities. From there, passengers continued to Johannesburg by road.

BOAC’s own newsletter of the time praised the dam as an ideal flying-boat station, with a usable area of about 90 square miles and alighting areas of 11,000 to 20,000 feet, and noted that it was almost unheard of for flying to be stopped by bad weather.

The end of an era

Flying boats were romantic, but they were slow. Pressurised land planes such as the Handley Page Hermes could fly London to Johannesburg in about a day and a half. In November 1950 BOAC replaced its Solent service with Hermes flights, and the last flying boats left the Vaal Dam.

What you can see today

The BOAC terminal building was later demolished. Because the dam wall has been raised since the 1940s, its concrete foundations now sit below the normal waterline at Deneysville, but they have been seen again when the dam drops very low, as it did in 2020. If you happen to visit in a dry spell, look out along the Deneysville shore, and please treat what you find as heritage.

Got a flying-boat memory? Old photographs, tickets and family stories from the Vaal Dam flying-boat years are rare. Share yours with us.
