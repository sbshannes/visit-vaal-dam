UJ Island and the graves beneath the Vaal Dam

A farm that became an island, a university that owns it, and the old headstones that rise from the water in times of drought.

The farm called Oase

Before the dam, the land near today’s Deneysville was farmland on the banks of the Vaal River. According to the University of Johannesburg’s own history of the island, it was once part of a farm called Oase, owned from 1902 to 1934 by Victor Edouard d’Assonville, the son of a French nobleman.

He and his wife, Maria, broke the stones themselves and carted them by ox wagon to build their stone homestead, on the spot where the university’s buildings stand today. The farm was so isolated that the family signalled across the river with mirrors, using an agreed number of flashes to send messages to Vaalbank on the Transvaal side, where the school was.

When the dam filled, the farm was expropriated and the high ground became a large island. It was government property until 1973. The surrounding area has a long history: a book by Prof. V.E. d’Assonville, Op die bodem van die Vaaldam, tells the story of the old farm Zandfontein and its families, whose homes, orchards and graves went under the rising water.

An island owned by a university

Today the island belongs to the University of Johannesburg, and was earlier known as the RAU Island after UJ’s predecessor, the Rand Afrikaans University. Locally it is also called Groot Eiland. UJ describes itself as the only university in the world that owns its own island.

The university follows a strict nature conservation policy on the island, and it supports a variety of plants, animals and birds.

Visiting the island

The island is not open to casual visitors: it is private University of Johannesburg property, and you reach it only by boat (a crossing of roughly 15 minutes) or helicopter. UJ offers organised group visits, which according to the university can include a boat trip around the island, a game drive and a hiking trail. Contact the University of Johannesburg directly to ask about group bookings.

The graves that rise from the water

When the Vaal Dam drops very low, something haunting appears on the island’s exposed shore: old farm graves, normally deep under water. Headstones have been photographed during the low levels of 2005, 2016 and 2024, and photos shared on social media in late 2020 appear to show them again.

Among the names recorded by genealogists is Jacobus Johannes Meyer, who died in 1893 (sources differ on his year of birth). Another stone reads, in Afrikaans, in memory of a beloved husband and father, Jacobus Frederik Janse van Vuuren (spelled “van Vuren” on the stone), born 17 February 1860, died 14 January 1928, just ten years before the dam was completed. The graves of Voortrekker families who settled here in the nineteenth century lie among them.

These were people who farmed this valley, raised families and buried their dead here, long before anyone imagined a lake. When the water falls, they quietly come back into view.

Fact-checkers at Africa Check confirmed in 2020 that the photographs are genuine and that the graves lie on the island, normally beneath the water.

Visit with respect

The graves lie on private UJ land and are only visible in drought years. Please visit only with the university’s permission, never disturb or remove anything, and treat the site as the cemetery it is. In December 2024 genealogists reported that at least one historic headstone, that of the Voortrekker Jacoba Roets (1825–1893), had gone missing from the site. Every stone that remains is part of the dam’s memory.

See the current dam level before planning a visit, and read about the flying-boat foundations that also surface at low water.
